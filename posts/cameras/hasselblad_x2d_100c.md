## Overview: a camera with the confidence to do less

Most $8,000 cameras try to do everything. The X2D 100C does the opposite, and that is the entire point of owning one. Hasselblad built a 100-megapixel medium-format stills camera and then deliberately left out the features that would dilute it. There is no fast burst mode worth mentioning, no clever animal-tracking autofocus, and effectively no video anyone should buy this camera to shoot. What you get instead is a sensor considerably larger than full-frame, 16-bit color depth, and a shooting experience engineered to slow you down. For a landscape photographer at the edge of a valley at dawn, or a portrait artist working a single face under controlled light, that focus is the appeal, not the compromise.

This review will not pretend the X2D is a versatile all-rounder, because it isn't, and Hasselblad never claimed it was. The right question is not "what can't it do" — the list is long — but "is what it does worth $8,199 to you." For a specific kind of photographer, the answer is genuinely yes. For most people it is emphatically no.

## Design and build: the object itself

Hasselblad's industrial design is the reason many buyers walk in the door, and the X2D delivers on it. The body is milled aluminum, weighs just under 900 grams with the battery, and feels denser and more deliberate than any plastic-shelled mirrorless camera. The grip is deep and beautifully sculpted. For scale, the sensor measures roughly 44 x 33mm — about 1.7x the area of a 36 x 24mm full-frame sensor — and captures 16-bit color at its native ISO 64 base. The card slot takes CFexpress, and the built-in SSD holds 1TB, enough for hundreds of those enormous 100MP files before you ever touch a card. The controls are sparse on purpose: two dials, a handful of buttons, and a gorgeous 3.6-inch tilting rear touchscreen paired with a 5.76-million-dot OLED viewfinder. The menu system is famously minimal, all clean typography and large touch targets, which is a relief after the labyrinthine menus of Japanese flagships.

Two engineering choices define the workflow. First, the storage: the X2D has a 1TB SSD built directly into the body, alongside a CFexpress card slot. That internal drive is fast and enormous — 100-megapixel 16-bit RAW files are large, and the SSD swallows them without a stutter — but it also means your images live inside the camera until you offload them. You tether or connect over USB-C to pull files, and if you are not disciplined about backing up, a single point of failure now holds your shoot. Second, the sensor sits on a 5-axis in-body stabilizer rated around 7 stops, which is unusual and valuable in medium format, because it lets you handhold a camera that historically lived on a tripod.

## Image quality is the whole argument

This is where the X2D justifies itself. The 100-megapixel sensor resolves detail that full-frame cannot approach, and the larger photosites paired with 16-bit capture produce tonal gradations — skies, skin, subtle color transitions — that look different in a way spec sheets struggle to convey. Hasselblad's Natural Color Solution renders color with a restraint and accuracy that many photographers describe as the closest thing to not having to color-grade at all. For fine-art printing at large sizes, for high-end commercial and portrait work, this is the payoff.

The leaf shutters built into Hasselblad's XCD lenses add a professional advantage: flash sync at very high shutter speeds, up to 1/2000 second with the right lens, which studio and location strobe shooters exploit to overpower the sun. Dynamic range is deep, in the neighborhood Hasselblad cites as 15 stops, giving you enormous latitude to shape a raw file. If your work ends as a large print or a billboard, the difference is not academic.

Consider what 100 megapixels means in practice: files large enough to print beyond 40 inches on the long edge at 300ppi without interpolation, with room to crop and still out-resolve a 24MP full-frame frame. Hasselblad quotes 15 stops of dynamic range, and while lab conditions never fully survive contact with a real landscape, the latitude to lift a shadow or hold a bright sky is visibly deeper than a smaller sensor delivers. The tradeoff is data: a single raw file can exceed 200MB, which is why the 1TB internal SSD and fast CFexpress cards are not luxuries but requirements.

## The pace, and the autofocus you must accept

Now the honest limitations. The X2D is slow, and not by accident. Autofocus is phase-detect and a real improvement over Hasselblad's contact-detect past, accurate and confident on static subjects, but it is nobody's idea of fast. It will not chase a running child, a bird, or a model moving quickly through a scene. There is no meaningful continuous-tracking sports mode. Continuous shooting is a trickle, on the order of a few frames per second, and the huge files mean the camera pauses to think more than a mirrorless shooter is used to.

Treat all of this as the design, because it is. The X2D wants you on a tripod or working deliberately, composing, refining, releasing the shutter once. Photographers who thrive in that rhythm — landscape, architecture, studio portrait, fine art — will not feel constrained. Photographers who need to react will be miserable, and should not be looking at this camera in the first place. There is a rhythm the X2D rewards: meter, compose, refine, breathe, release. If that description makes you exhale, this camera is speaking your language. If it makes you impatient, you have your answer, and no spec on the box will change it.

And the video? Skip it. This is not a video camera in any serious sense, and no one should buy an X2D to shoot motion. If moving images matter to your work at all, buy something else and keep the Hasselblad for stills, or buy something else entirely.

## How it compares: GFX100 II, GFX100S II, and full-frame high-resolution

The obvious rivals come from Fujifilm's GFX line, which uses a similar-sized medium-format sensor. The GFX100 II is the versatile counterpoint: it has strong subject-detection autofocus, fast bursts, and genuinely capable video, and it costs less while doing far more of everything. If you want a 100-megapixel camera that can also shoot action and video, the GFX100 II is the smarter, more flexible buy, full stop. The GFX100S II is cheaper still and offers most of that resolution in a lighter body for photographers who want the sensor without the flagship price.

So why choose the Hasselblad? For the things Fujifilm does not sell: the color science, the tactile object, the leaf-shutter flash sync, the pared-down interface, and the sheer pleasure of the shooting experience. It is an emotional and aesthetic purchase as much as a technical one, and that is a legitimate reason to buy — as long as you know that is what you are doing. Photographers who only care about resolution per dollar should also weigh a full-frame 61-megapixel body like the Sony A7R V, which costs a fraction as much and prints beautifully, if not to medium-format standards.

## Who should buy it, and who should absolutely not

Buy the X2D 100C if you are a landscape, architecture, studio-portrait, or fine-art photographer who prints large, if color rendering and tonal depth are worth a premium to you, and if you find deliberate, unhurried shooting a feature rather than a frustration. It is a tool for people who already know exactly why they want it.

Do not buy it if you shoot anything that moves, if you need video, if you want one camera for many jobs, or if the internal-SSD workflow and disciplined offloading it demands sound like a hassle. Do not buy it as your only camera. And do not buy it hoping the 100 megapixels will fix your photography — this rewards craft you already have, and punishes impatience.

## Verdict

The X2D 100C is a beautiful, narrow instrument. Within its lane — static, considered, large-format stills — it produces images with a color and tonal quality that justify the price to the photographers who need them. Outside that lane it is slow, single-minded, and no bargain, and a Fujifilm GFX100 II will run circles around it in versatility. This is not a camera you compromise toward; it is one you choose because its exact limitations match your exact way of working. If they do, few cameras will make you happier. If they don't, the price is a very expensive lesson.

## FAQ

### Can the X2D 100C shoot video for events or interviews?

No, and you should not plan on it. This is a stills camera through and through, with no serious video capability, and Hasselblad does not position it as a hybrid tool. If you need moving images, buy a different camera and reserve the X2D for photographs.

### What happens to my images if the internal 1TB SSD fails?

That is the real risk of the built-in drive design. Files live inside the camera until you offload them over USB-C or a card, so an undisciplined workflow leaves a single point of failure. Back up after every shoot, use the CFexpress slot in parallel where possible, and never treat the SSD as long-term storage.

### Is the autofocus fast enough for portraits of moving subjects?

For a posed portrait, yes — it locks accurately on a still or slowly shifting subject. For a subject moving quickly, no. There is no capable continuous-tracking mode, so anything approaching action photography is outside what this camera can do.

### Why buy this over a Fujifilm GFX100 II that does more for less?

Only for what Fujifilm doesn't offer: Hasselblad's color science, the machined body and shooting feel, leaf-shutter flash sync to 1/2000 second, and the minimalist interface. If versatility and value matter more than those things, the GFX100 II is genuinely the better buy.

### Do I need a tripod to get the most out of it?

Not strictly, because the roughly 7-stop in-body stabilization makes handholding surprisingly viable for a 100-megapixel medium-format camera. But to fully exploit the resolution for landscape and architecture, a tripod and careful technique still pay off.

### How large can I actually print these files?

Very large. The 100-megapixel, 16-bit files hold detail and tonal smoothness that support gallery-scale prints and billboard reproduction, which is exactly the use case that justifies the sensor and the price.

### What are the hidden costs of this system?

Budget beyond the body. XCD lenses are premium-priced, fast CFexpress cards and reliable backup storage are essential given the workflow, and the leaf-shutter lenses that enable high-speed flash sync add up. This is an expensive system to build out, not just an expensive body.

### Is 100 megapixels overkill for most photographers?

For most people, yes. If your images end up on screens or small prints, you will not see the benefit and the file sizes will slow your workflow. The resolution only earns its keep for large-format printing and demanding commercial and fine-art work.
