## Introduction

The Anker SOLIX F3800 is a portable power station only in the same sense that a compact
generator is portable: it can move, but it is not something most people will casually
carry. Its 3,840Wh LiFePO4 battery sits behind a 6,000W inverter, a 9,000W surge rating,
and native 120V/240V output. That combination makes the F3800 interesting for well pumps,
workshop tools, RV service, and selected home circuits rather than merely phones and
laptops.

Anker's official SOLIX announcement page is dated January 8, 2024. The product is no
longer new, but its central proposition has aged well: a single wheeled unit can handle
loads that expose the limits of ordinary 2,000W or 3,000W stations. The more important
question in 2026 is whether the F3800's current $1,999.99 price and awkward solar-input
design still make sense beside newer systems.

This review separates Anker's specifications from The Solar Lab's independent measurements.
It does not claim hands-on testing. The conclusion is narrow: the F3800 is a strong
high-output backup platform when 240V power or motor-starting ability matters, but it is
an expensive and heavy choice if your real need is quiet, light-duty energy storage.

## Design, Build, and Setup

At about 132 pounds, the F3800 is a roll-between-rooms machine. The wheels and telescoping
handle help on a garage floor, driveway, or other smooth surface, but they do not solve
stairs, a truck-bed lift, or uneven ground. Plan where it will live before buying it.
The weight is not a cosmetic drawback: a station this heavy is less likely to be moved
into position when an outage arrives.

The chassis and port layout are built around large loads. Anker lists both 120V and 240V
output, a 50A receptacle, and a transfer-switch-friendly connection for home integration.
That is materially different from a typical portable station with a handful of 120V
outlets. It also means setup deserves electrical planning. A transfer switch or approved
inlet is required for connecting backup power to household circuits; plugging a generator
cord into a wall outlet is dangerous backfeeding, not an installation method.

The front display and Anker app expose the usual battery, input, and output information.
Wi-Fi and Bluetooth monitoring is useful when the F3800 is parked in a utility area, and
the display is more practical than a tiny status light when several high-draw appliances
are running. The tradeoff is a platform with more cables, switches, and installation
decisions than a small camping station.

## Battery and Output Performance

The F3800's 3,840Wh nameplate capacity is respectable, but its inverter is the reason to
buy it. Anker rates continuous output at 6,000W and surge at 9,000W. In plain terms, it
has far more room for starting a compressor, pump, lift, or power tool than a unit whose
inverter tops out at 2,000W.

The Solar Lab's load tests put useful limits around those ratings. Its sample ran a
5,100W load for about 37 minutes, which worked out to approximately 84% efficiency from
battery to the outlets under that test condition. That is an independent result from one
review setup, not a universal runtime figure, but it is a meaningful indication that
heavy output comes with conversion losses. A 3,000W load would theoretically empty the
base pack in a little over an hour before losses; actual runtime depends on reserve,
temperature, and load cycling.

The same testing found that the unit could sustain 7,200W for roughly 10 seconds and
8,000W for only about 2 to 3 seconds. That is still useful surge behavior, especially for
brief motor starts, but it is not permission to treat 9,000W as continuous output. Buyers
should compare the startup requirement of a specific pump or compressor with the F3800's
ratings instead of assuming every appliance will start.

The unit also handled a demanding workshop test in The Solar Lab's evaluation, including
a 3,000W air compressor and a 2,200W car lift. Those are valuable examples because they
show what the large inverter is for. They are not guarantees for every motor, since
startup current varies with voltage, temperature, wiring, and the appliance's controller.

## Charging and the Solar Limitation

Anker says the F3800 can recharge from a 120V AC outlet in about 2 hours and 25 minutes.
The Solar Lab measured about 2 hours and 35 minutes in its AC charging test, a small
difference that supports the claim under that reviewer's conditions. The unit supports
solar pass-through, so it can run a load while solar input is replenishing the battery.

The solar specification looks excellent at first: up to 2,400W. The catch is that the
F3800 uses two solar inputs, each with a 32V-to-60V operating range and a 25A maximum.
The Solar Lab found that its conventional panel setup reached about 1,200W rather than
the advertised ceiling. Its explanation was electrical rather than mysterious: common
panels and wiring make it difficult to use the full power without exceeding the input
voltage or current limits.

That does not make the solar system useless. A 1,200W harvest is still substantial for
an outage kit, and Anker's own compatible panel configurations may perform differently.
It does mean the headline 2,400W number should be treated as a best-case design limit,
not as a result every owner can obtain by connecting a large rooftop array. Check panel
voltage, current, and series/parallel wiring before buying this station for solar-first
off-grid use.

There is another important constraint: Anker's product documentation says the 240V output
cannot be used while the F3800 is charging from 120V AC. That matters for a home-backup
plan that expects to charge and feed a 240V circuit simultaneously. The F3800 is still
flexible, but its operating modes are not interchangeable.

## Expansion and Home Backup

The F3800's base battery is only the beginning of its intended system. Anker describes
expansion to 26.9kWh with additional batteries, enough to turn a short outage buffer into
a multi-day essential-load reserve. Expansion makes more sense when the F3800 is installed
in one place; adding batteries to a 132-pound base unit is not a casual camping upgrade.

The 240V output is the other major system feature. It can support appliances and circuits
that a 120V-only station cannot, but the presence of a 240V receptacle does not eliminate
installation requirements. Connecting to a home's panel calls for a compatible transfer
device, code-compliant wiring, and often an electrician. Budget for those parts before
comparing the F3800's price with a smaller plug-in station.

The result is best thought of as a quiet, fuel-free generator substitute for selected
loads. It can run a refrigerator, freezer, networking equipment, lights, tools, or a
properly sized pump; it cannot provide unlimited whole-home energy from 3,840Wh. A
6,000W inverter determines what can run at once, while battery capacity determines how
long it can run. Expansion improves the second constraint, not the physical weight.

## Anker SOLIX F3800 vs. the Alternatives

**EcoFlow DELTA Pro Ultra.** EcoFlow's larger whole-home platform is the closer match for
buyers who prioritize a broader integrated ecosystem and more modular home-storage
options. The F3800 is simpler as a single high-output box and typically easier to justify
for workshop or RV use. Choose EcoFlow when you are building a larger permanent system;
choose Anker when the single-unit 6,000W/240V capability is the central requirement.

**Bluetti AC300 with B300 batteries.** Bluetti's modular design starts with a separate
power module and battery, which can be easier to expand or replace piece by piece. The
F3800 is less modular at the base but cleaner to deploy as one wheeled appliance. Buyers
who want a configurable battery architecture should investigate Bluetti; buyers who want
one integrated high-output unit may prefer Anker.

**Goal Zero Yeti PRO 4000.** Goal Zero's Yeti PRO 4000 is another large LiFePO4 station
aimed at backup and off-grid users. It is a credible alternative for buyers already in
Goal Zero's ecosystem, but the F3800's native 240V output and 6,000W inverter are the
more compelling specifications for heavy household loads. The Yeti is more attractive
when its accessory network or a different installation plan matters more than peak output.

**Anker SOLIX F3800 Plus.** The newer F3800 Plus is the obvious in-family comparison. It
raises solar input to 3,200W and keeps the 6,000W class output, so it is the better fit
for a new solar-primary installation if the price difference is reasonable. The original
F3800 remains compelling when discounted and when its 2,400W solar ceiling is sufficient.
Do not buy the older model solely because its name looks similar to the Plus.

## Who Should Buy It, and Who Should Skip It?

Buy the F3800 if you need to start demanding motors, supply 240V power, or build a
home-backup system around a high-output base unit. It is especially defensible for a
workshop, RV owner, or homeowner with a defined transfer-switch plan. The independent
84% heavy-load efficiency result and successful compressor and lift tests give the
specification some useful context beyond the box.

Skip it if your outage plan is a refrigerator, router, lights, and phone charging for a
few hours. A smaller 1,500Wh-to-2,000Wh LiFePO4 station will be easier to move and may
leave money for a second battery or solar panel. Skip it as well if you need a solar
array that can easily deliver the full advertised input, because the two 60V channels
may force an inconvenient panel design.

## Verdict

The Anker SOLIX F3800 is still a serious piece of backup hardware. Its 6,000W inverter,
9,000W surge rating, 240V output, and expansion path solve problems that ordinary
portable stations cannot. The Solar Lab's measurements show both why it is useful under
heavy loads and why buyers should not read the marketing numbers as unlimited output.

The decisive compromise is that the F3800 is much better at making power than at making
solar integration easy. Add the 132-pound chassis and the fact that 240V cannot operate
while charging from 120V AC, and this becomes a specialist purchase rather than a
universal recommendation. Buy it when high-output, 240V backup is the job. If the job is
simply keeping electronics and a refrigerator alive, spend less and carry less.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When did the Anker SOLIX F3800 launch?

Anker's official SOLIX F3800 announcement page is dated January 8, 2024. That is the
primary-source launch/announcement date used here, rather than the discovery article's
publication date.

### How much does the F3800 cost?

Anker's current US product page lists the F3800 at $1,999.99. Prices and bundles change,
and the base station is not the complete cost of a transfer-switch or expanded-battery
installation.

### Can one F3800 provide 240V?

Yes. Anker lists dual-voltage 120V/240V output from the unit. That capability is intended
for appropriate appliances or a properly installed home-backup connection, not for
backfeeding a wall outlet.

### Can it power a well pump?

It may, depending on the pump's running and startup requirements. The 6,000W inverter and
9,000W surge rating give it more headroom than most portable stations, but check the
pump's locked-rotor amps and compare them with Anker's output limits before relying on it.

### How much energy does the base battery store?

The base battery is rated at 3,840Wh. Real outlet energy is lower because the inverter
uses power during conversion, and a motor or heating load can consume that reserve quickly.

### How much solar input can it accept?

Anker specifies up to 2,400W through two solar inputs. Each input operates within a
32V-to-60V range and has a 25A maximum, so the panel configuration matters more than the
headline wattage. The Solar Lab reached about 1,200W with its panel setup.

### Can it charge and run appliances at the same time?

Solar pass-through is supported. However, Anker says the 240V output cannot be used while
the unit is charging from 120V AC, so do not assume every output mode works during every
charging mode.

### How long will it run a refrigerator?

There is no single honest answer because refrigerators cycle rather than drawing their
maximum continuously. A 3,840Wh battery can cover a refrigerator and small essentials
for a substantial outage, but actual runtime depends on the refrigerator, ambient
temperature, inverter settings, and what else is connected.

### How heavy is the F3800?

It weighs about 132 pounds. One person can roll it on a smooth surface with the built-in
wheels, but lifting it into a vehicle or up stairs should be treated as a two-person job.

### Can the battery capacity be expanded?

Yes. Anker describes expansion to 26.9kWh with additional batteries. Expansion increases
runtime; it does not make the base chassis lighter or remove the need for compatible
hardware and cables.

### Does it need an electrician?

The station can power appliances directly without an electrician. Connecting it to a
house panel requires a compatible transfer device and code-compliant installation, which
normally means consulting a licensed electrician.

### Is it a true UPS?

It includes UPS functionality on supported output paths, but buyers who need a specific
computer-grade transfer time should verify the current documentation for their exact
configuration. A dedicated UPS remains the safer choice for sensitive desktops and NAS
systems when a published switchover figure is essential.

### Should I buy the original F3800 or the F3800 Plus?

Choose the Plus when higher solar input is central to the plan. Choose the original when
its lower price is meaningful and a 2,400W solar ceiling, 6,000W output, and 240V
capability cover the loads you actually need. The older model is not automatically a
better value if you will spend heavily trying to work around its solar-input limits.
